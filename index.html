How the mazes are carved

A maze generator usually runs one classic algorithm, once, and prints what comes out. Ours works in stages, and most of the work goes into the parts you do not see at first.

  1. The way through comes first.

    The engine lays out the solution before anything else, and it makes the path wander into every region of the drawing instead of cutting across it.

  2. Dead ends are carved as mazes of their own.

    Each one gets its own branches. They are carved in the same hand as the true path, so at a glance nothing tells them apart. Large ones take up the space. Small ones blur their edges.

  3. In the larger mazes, some dead ends loop.

    You can walk around one for a long while before you notice you are back where you started. Each loop gets as many forks as the rest of the maze, so it does not stand out.

  4. Hard, without the busywork.

    A long solution is easy to make and tiring to follow, so the engine does not reward length. It scores each maze on how far the path roams, how often it offers a real choice, and how deep a wrong choice takes you before you find out. Dead ends you can rule out without lifting the pencil count against a maze.

  5. Twenty candidates, one kept.

    Every maze here was carved twenty times, with a different result each time. The candidates are scored against each other and the best one is kept. If none of them clears the bar, the whole batch is thrown out and carved again.

  6. One way through, checked.

    Before a maze is published, the engine checks every step of the solution: take that step away and the entrance is cut off from the exit. That is what keeps the solution single, loops included.

Each setting behind these steps was tuned by hand: how long a corridor runs before it turns, how big a dead end may grow. Each one was set by carving sample mazes, measuring them and looking at them, then set again. Pick a tiling above and print one.

Questions

Are these mazes free?

Yes. All 24 mazes are free to download and print, as PDF and SVG, for personal and classroom use.

Can I use them in class?

Yes. Print as many copies as your students need. Please do not resell them.

What paper size do they print on?

Each PDF has two A4 pages: the maze, then its solution. On US Letter, print with “fit to page”. The SVG scales to any size.

Does every maze have only one solution?

Yes. Every maze is checked before it is published: each step of the solution is a bridge, so there is no second way through.

What is an aperiodic tiling?

A way to cover the plane with tiles so that the pattern never repeats, however far you shift it. Four tilings here are aperiodic: the hat, the spectre, Penrose rhombi and the pinwheel.

What is the hat tiling?

A tiling made from copies of a single tile shaped like a hat. It covers the whole plane, some copies are mirror images, and the pattern never repeats.

What is the spectre tile?

A single tile with fourteen sides of equal length. It covers the whole plane without mirror copies, and the pattern never repeats.

What is a Penrose tiling?

A tiling made from two rhombi, a thin one and a thick one, fitted together with five-fold symmetry so that the pattern never repeats.

How are the mazes made?

Our own engine carves each maze in two passes. First the solution path, then the dead ends, each carved as a small maze of its own. Some dead ends close into loops. Twenty candidates are carved for every maze and scored, and the best one is kept.

Are there bigger mazes?

Yes. The free mazes stop at about 1,200 cells. The Unsquare Mazes book picks up from there: fifty mazes of about 1,000 to 2,400 cells, one per page.

The book: 50 large mazes on eight tilings
